\begin{abstract}
Educational data mining has made substantial progress in predicting student academic performance, yet most studies remain focused on risk identification rather than model-based recourse design. Explainable AI methods can highlight influential predictors, but they do not necessarily specify which changes are causally meaningful, practically feasible, and equitable across student groups. This study proposes a DAG-informed actionable algorithmic recourse framework that moves beyond prediction-oriented early warning toward actionability-constrained educational decision support.

Using the UCI Student Performance dataset (\textit{student-por.csv}, $N=649$), we evaluated student performance prediction without final grades ($G1/G2$) \hlbox{using 5-fold stratified cross-validation} (Accuracy: \hlbox{$0.8335 \pm 0.0156$}, ROC AUC: \hlbox{$0.7263 \pm 0.0556$}, Class Prevalence: $84.6\%$ pass, $15.4\%$ fail). We then implemented an actionability- and DAG-constrained recourse optimization pipeline across 10 random seeds to generate counterfactual recommendations under discrete UCI scale boundaries. Socioeconomic status (SES) was operationalized via a composite index combining parental education, family size, and digital access.

Across multi-seed evaluation, the proposed framework produced valid recourse recommendations for an average of $11.5 \text{ out of } 12.1$ predicted failing students per test fold (validity rate \hlbox{$95.5\% \pm 4.7\%$}) while restricting interventions to plausible academic levers (e.g., non-decreasing study time and non-increasing absenteeism) drawn from a sampled directional candidate grid ($K=700$) of the full $9,400$-point discrete space $\mathcal{F}_{\text{discrete}}$. Average recourse distances under group-independent evaluation metrics were \hlbox{$\mu_L = 0.3408 \pm 0.1441$} for Low-SES students and \hlbox{$\mu_H = 0.2638 \pm 0.1633$} for High-SES students (seed-indexed \hlbox{$\text{RFD} = \mathbb{E}[|\mu_{L,s} - \mu_{H,s}|] = 0.1613 \pm 0.1038$}, macro group difference $|\bar{\mu}_L - \bar{\mu}_H| = 0.0771$). Furthermore, recommendations shifted model predictions, increasing the average model-predicted passing probability for failing students from \hlbox{$0.2866$ before recourse} to \hlbox{$0.5975$ after recourse}.

\hlblock{Constrained search raises recourse distance relative to unconstrained search while keeping high validity, and the observed probability shift is model-based decision-boundary crossing rather than evidence of educational effectiveness. Fairness weighting did not reduce group-independent distance beyond actionability constraints alone. Overall, model-based recourse in education highlights the trade-off between unconstrained optimization and actionable feasibility, demonstrating that responsible educational AI must evaluate the realistic constraints of recommended success pathways.}
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} educational data mining; student performance prediction; algorithmic recourse; actionable counterfactuals; decision-boundary reversal; educational decision support.

\section{Introduction}

Predicting student academic performance has become one of the central problems in educational data mining and learning analytics, largely because early identification of at-risk learners can support timely academic support, targeted advising, and institutional support. Recent review studies show that student performance prediction has evolved into a mature research area in which machine learning and data mining techniques are routinely used to estimate achievement, failure risk, or dropout-related outcomes across diverse educational settings \cite{Albreiki2021,Zhang2021}. At the same time, this literature has increasingly emphasized that prediction is not an end in itself; rather, its educational value lies in whether it can support meaningful action for student success.

As predictive models became more accurate, the field also moved toward explainability. Post hoc explanation techniques such as LIME and SHAP have been widely adopted to clarify which variables most strongly influence model outputs and to make educational predictions more transparent for instructors and stakeholders \cite{Ribeiro2016,Lundberg2017}. In educational contexts, this shift is important because high-performing black-box models are often difficult to justify when used in sensitive decisions affecting student trajectories. However, explainability alone does not resolve the core practical question faced by educators and students: what should be changed, concretely and feasibly, for a student to improve the predicted outcome? Feature attribution methods can indicate influential variables, but they do not by themselves guarantee that changing those variables would causally improve academic performance.

This limitation points to an important conceptual transition from prediction to model-based recourse. Much of the current student-performance literature remains diagnostic: it identifies who is likely to fail and which factors are associated with risk, but it rarely provides personalized, operational, and evidence-grounded recommendations for reversing that risk. In machine learning, this problem is closely related to algorithmic recourse, which refers to the possibility of changing an unfavorable prediction by modifying actionable input features \cite{Ustun2019}. The recourse literature emerged precisely because explanations are often insufficient when decision subjects need actionable guidance rather than retrospective interpretation. More recent surveys and methodological work further distinguish counterfactual explanations from true recourse, emphasizing that actionable recommendations should be realistic, feasible, and aligned with how the world actually changes \cite{Karimi2021,Karimi2022,Verma2024}.

A second major limitation concerns the difference between correlation and causation. In educational datasets, student achievement is shaped by tightly interdependent academic, behavioral, and socioeconomic variables. Attendance, study time, parental education, prior achievement, digital access, and financial constraints do not operate independently; they affect one another through structural pathways. For this reason, a model that merely identifies correlates of failure does not justify causal action claims such as ``reducing absenteeism will improve performance'' or ``increasing study hours is the minimal effective action.'' Karimi et al. \cite{Karimi2021} explicitly argue that recourse should not be based on nearest counterfactual changes alone, because such suggestions may violate causal dependencies and therefore produce unrealistic or invalid recommendations. In high-stakes settings such as education, this makes a causal perspective essential.

The issue becomes even more critical when fairness is considered. In many educational AI studies, fairness is evaluated at the prediction layer through performance parity or error-rate comparisons across demographic groups. While this is necessary, it is not sufficient. A model may appear fair in its outputs while still prescribing highly unequal paths to success. For example, a recommendation such as ``reduce absenteeism'' may be far easier for a high-socioeconomic-status student than for a student who must work, commute long distances, or carry family responsibilities. Likewise, increasing weekly study time may impose very different burdens depending on gendered care roles or access to learning resources. Causal fairness research has long argued that fairness must be evaluated in counterfactual terms rather than through surface-level statistical parity alone \cite{Kusner2017}. Recent recourse research similarly highlights that model-based recourse pathways themselves can embed inequity if the feasibility and cost of action are not explicitly modeled \cite{vonKugelgen2022}.

These concerns motivate a shift from general fairness toward fair recourse in education. The core idea is that a responsible student-support model should not only identify actionable changes, but should do so in a way that respects students' structural constraints and does not systematically burden disadvantaged groups with harder or more expensive recourse requirements. In other words, the question is no longer only whether a model predicts fairly, but whether the recommended route to a favorable outcome is equitably attainable. This distinction is especially important in educational settings, where institutional decisions may shape students' long-term opportunities and where model-based recourse advice can directly influence guidance, remediation, and support allocation.

Against this background, this study proposes a DAG-informed actionability-constrained recourse framework for student academic performance prediction. Rather than stopping at risk prediction or feature-level explanation, the proposed framework aims to evaluate whether recommended recourse paths are equitably attainable under structural constraints; empirically, on this dataset, fairness weighting was not more equitable than directional actionability constraints alone. By combining student-performance modeling with causal structure and recourse analysis, the study addresses three gaps simultaneously: (i)~the gap between prediction and prescription, (ii)~the gap between correlational explanation and causal action, and (iii)~the gap between predictive fairness and recourse attainability. \section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Problem formulation}
\label{subsec:problem_formulation}

Let $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the feature vector for student $i$, and let $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ denote the binary academic outcome ($y_i=1$ pass, $y_i=0$ fail). A predictive model $f(\mathbf{x})$ estimates the probability of success:
\begin{equation}
\hat{p}_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i) = P(Y=1 \mid \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}_i).
\end{equation}

For a student predicted to fail ($\hat{p}_i < \tau$, threshold $\tau=0.50$), the goal is to find a recourse vector $\boldsymbol{\delta}_i$ such that $\mathbf{x}_i' = \mathbf{x}_i + \boldsymbol{\delta}_i$ satisfies $f(\mathbf{x}_i') \ge \tau$ while minimizing recourse cost and respecting directional and feasibility constraints.

\hlblock{The framework implements DAG-constrained / actionability-constrained recourse under an assumed educational causal graph. It is not a fully identified Structural Causal Model (SCM) with estimated structural equations.}

\subsection{Dataset and primary composite SES construction}
\label{subsec:data}

The empirical evaluation uses the \textit{Student Performance} dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository (\textit{student-por.csv}, Portuguese course, $N=649$) \cite{CortezSilva2008,UCIStudentPerformance}. The target variable $y$ is defined as pass = 1 if final grade $G3 \ge 10$ and fail = 0 otherwise.

Variables were partitioned into three categories:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Actionable features}: \textit{studytime} (ordinal 1--4), \textit{absences} (integer 0--93), \textit{freetime} (ordinal 1--5), and \textit{goout} (ordinal 1--5).
    \item \textbf{Immutable features}: \textit{age} and prior academic failures (\textit{failures}).
    \item \textbf{Sensitive attribute}: Socioeconomic Status (SES).
\end{enumerate}

To align with domain literature, the primary SES variable was constructed as an equally weighted composite index:
\begin{equation}
\text{Composite Score}_i = \text{Medu}_i + \text{Fedu}_i + \mathbb{I}(\text{famsize}_i = \text{`LE3'}) + \mathbb{I}(\text{internet}_i = \text{`yes'}),
\end{equation}
where $\text{famsize\_small} = 1$ if family size $\le 3$ and $\text{internet\_yes} = 1$ if internet access is available at home. Students were split at the median score into Low SES ($\text{SES}=0$, $N=307$) and High SES ($\text{SES}=1$, $N=342$).

\subsection{Causal DAG assumptions and edge justifications}
\label{subsec:causal_dag}

Recourse recommendations are constrained by an assumed educational Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:dag}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{sci_fig0_causal_dag.png}
    \caption{Assumed educational causal DAG. Directed edges represent domain-informed structural dependencies based on educational literature. DAG parent restrictions mean immutable/sensitive features remain fixed while only actionable features vary under directional constraints. Structural equations are not estimated, and downstream $do()$ propagation is not performed.}
    \label{fig:dag}
\end{figure}

The educational DAG (Figure~\ref{fig:dag}) encodes 10 structural directed edges derived from established educational literature:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\text{SES} \to \text{studytime}$: Socioeconomic status determines home learning environment, study resources, and academic support access \cite{Sirin2005}.
    \item $\text{SES} \to \text{absences}$: Low-SES students face higher structural friction (long commutes, family responsibilities, health barriers) leading to attendance challenges \cite{OECD2018}.
    \item $\text{SES} \to \text{freetime}$: SES background influences extracurricular commitments and discretionary time allocation \cite{Sirin2005}.
    \item $\text{age} \to \text{goout}$: Student age shapes peer socialization patterns and out-of-school social activities \cite{Castro2015}.
    \item $\text{freetime} \to \text{goout}$: Available discretionary free time directly enables peer social outings \cite{Castro2015}.
    \item $\text{studytime} \to y$: Weekly study time is a direct behavioral determinant of academic preparation and subject pass probability $y$ ($G3 \ge 10$) \cite{Plant2005,Nonis2010}.
    \item $\text{absences} \to y$: Chronic absenteeism directly reduces classroom instructional exposure, impairing academic performance \cite{Gottfried2014,Gershenson2019}.
    \item $\text{freetime} \to y$: Free time management influences academic preparation balance \cite{Nonis2010}.
    \item $\text{goout} \to y$: Excessive social outings reduce study concentration and academic achievement \cite{Plant2005}.
    \item $\text{failures} \to y$: Prior academic failures directly reduce subject mastery and lower binary passing probability $y$ ($G3 \ge 10$) \cite{CortezSilva2008}.
\end{enumerate}

Exogenous background variables (sensitive attribute $\text{SES}$, immutable features $\text{age}$ and prior $\text{failures}$) remain fixed without incoming edges from actionable features. Note: $\text{SES}$ is parameterised as an exogenous composite index of household digital access, parental education, and family size constraints; it is not modeled as a causal effect of its own defining indicators, preventing circularity. Target node is binary pass outcome $y \in \{0,1\}$ ($G3 \ge 10$). Structural edge dependencies are defined in the educational DAG, but downstream $do()$ propagation is not performed.

\hlblock{DAG parent restrictions mean immutable/sensitive features remain fixed while only actionable features vary under directional constraints. Structural equations are not estimated, and downstream $do()$ propagation is not performed.}

\subsection{Historical absences proxy disclaimer}
\label{subsec:absences_proxy}

\hlblock{Recorded absences in the dataset represent historical counts. Recommended reductions are simulated hypothetical changes to a future-attendance / engagement proxy, not a retrospective rewrite of past absences. Prediction is assumed to be issued mid-term / before final outcome, but the UCI snapshot does not encode this timing. Therefore absences are treated as a constrained actionable proxy, not a directly implementable past intervention.}

\subsection{Mathematical optimization formulation and discrete candidate space}
\label{subsec:optimization}

The full discrete feature space spans $\mathcal{F}_{\text{discrete}} = \mathcal{X}_{\text{study}} \times \mathcal{X}_{\text{abs}} \times \mathcal{X}_{\text{free}} \times \mathcal{X}_{\text{go}} = \{1,2,3,4\} \times \{0,1,\dots,93\} \times \{1,2,3,4,5\} \times \{1,2,3,4,5\}$, yielding a total discrete space of $|\mathcal{F}_{\text{discrete}}| = 4 \times 94 \times 5 \times 5 = 9,400$ candidate points. For each student $i$ predicted to fail ($f(\mathbf{x}_i) < \tau=0.50$), counterfactual recommendations are drawn from a student-specific candidate grid of $K=700$ actionable candidate vectors constructed relative to initial feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i$ under directional actionability bounds ($\delta_{\text{study}} \ge 0, \delta_{\text{abs}} \le 0$).

We evaluate five recourse conditions:

1. \textbf{Unconstrained discrete search (DiCE-style)}:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\boldsymbol{\delta}} \mathcal{C}_{\text{unweighted}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f(\mathbf{x}_i + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \ge \tau, \quad (\mathbf{x}_i + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \in \mathcal{F}_{\text{discrete}}
\end{equation}
where features may shift in any direction under unweighted distance $\mathcal{C}_{\text{unweighted}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}) = \sum_{j \in \text{actionable}} \lambda_j |\delta_j|$.

2. \textbf{Actionability-constrained search (Ustun-aligned directional bounds)}:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\boldsymbol{\delta}} \mathcal{C}_{\text{unweighted}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f(\mathbf{x}_i + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \ge \tau, \quad \delta_{\text{study}} \ge 0, \quad \delta_{\text{abs}} \le 0, \quad (\mathbf{x}_i + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \in \mathcal{F}_{\text{discrete}}
\end{equation}
enforcing directional actionability bounds (non-decreasing study time and non-increasing absences).

3. \textbf{Fairness-weighted discrete search}:
Enforces actionability constraints while incorporating domain difficulty weights $w_j$ ($w_{\text{study}}=1.0, w_{\text{abs}}=1.5, w_{\text{free}}=1.0, w_{\text{go}}=1.0$) during candidate scoring:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\boldsymbol{\delta}} \mathcal{C}_{\text{domain}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f(\mathbf{x}_i + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \ge \tau, \quad \delta_{\text{study}} \ge 0, \quad \delta_{\text{abs}} \le 0, \quad (\mathbf{x}_i + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \in \mathcal{F}_{\text{discrete}}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}_{\text{domain}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}) = \sum_{j \in \text{actionable}} w_j \lambda_j |\delta_j|$ and bounds normalization factors are $\lambda_{\text{study}}=1/3, \lambda_{\text{abs}}=1/93, \lambda_{\text{free}}=1/4, \lambda_{\text{go}}=1/4$. Note: Fairness-weighted discrete search incorporates directional actionability bounds ($\delta_{\text{study}} \ge 0, \delta_{\text{abs}} \le 0$); an unconstrained fairness-only variant was not implemented as actionability constraints are prerequisite in educational recourse.

4. \textbf{Proposed SES-Sensitive Difficulty-Weighted Recourse}:
Joint actionability constraints and candidate scoring incorporating structural attendance friction weights for Low-SES students ($w_{\text{abs}}^{\text{Low}}=2.0$):
\begin{equation}
\min_{\boldsymbol{\delta}} \mathcal{C}_{\text{scoring}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}, \text{SES}_i) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f(\mathbf{x}_i + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \ge \tau, \quad \delta_{\text{study}} \ge 0, \quad \delta_{\text{abs}} \le 0, \quad (\mathbf{x}_i + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \in \mathcal{F}_{\text{discrete}}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}_{\text{scoring}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}, \text{SES}_i) = \sum_{j \in \text{actionable}} w_j(\text{SES}_i) \lambda_j |\delta_j|$, with $w_{\text{abs}}(0) = 2.0$ and $w_{\text{abs}}(1) = 1.0$.

5. \textbf{Manifold nearest-neighbor recourse baseline}:
Finds the nearest passing instance in the training set graph manifold satisfying directional actionability bounds ($\delta_{\text{study}} \ge 0, \delta_{\text{abs}} \le 0$), evaluated over all passing instances in the training fold ($N_{\text{pass,train}} \approx 439$). The manifold baseline evaluates all $N_{\text{pass,train}} \approx 439$ passing training instances, whereas discrete search evaluates a student-specific candidate grid of $K=700$ directional vectors.

\textbf{Candidate Selection and Tie-Breaking Rule}: If multiple valid candidate vectors satisfy $f(\mathbf{x}_i + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \ge \tau$ with identical minimal scoring cost, candidate selection breaks ties deterministically by selecting the candidate with (i)~smallest study time increase $\delta_{\text{study}}$, (ii)~smallest absolute absence change $|\delta_{\text{abs}}|$, and (iii)~first index in the pre-generated candidate grid.

\textbf{Difficulty-Weighted Recourse Clarification}: The proposed method functions as an asymmetric feature difficulty-weighted recourse model where $w_{\text{abs}}(\text{Low})=2.0$ reflects structural attendance friction, rather than a group-inequality penalty term optimized in the loss objective.

\subsection{Fairness evaluation metrics and seed-indexed RFD formulation}
\label{subsec:metrics}

To evaluate all methods on an unbiased, group-independent benchmark scale, evaluation cost is defined as pure normalized feature shift distance:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{C}_{\text{eval}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}) &= \sum_{j \in \text{actionable}} \lambda_j |\delta_j| \\
\text{RFD} &= \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S} |\mu_{L,s} - \mu_{H,s}| \\
\Delta_g &= \mu_g^{\text{base}} - \mu_g^{\text{method}} \\
\text{RBR}_L &= \frac{\mu_L^{\text{base}} - \mu_L^{\text{method}}}{\mu_L^{\text{base}}} \times 100\%
\end{align}
where $s \in \{1, \dots, S\}$ indexes evaluation splits (seeds, $S=10$), and $\mu_{L,s}$ and $\mu_{H,s}$ denote the mean group recourse costs within fold $s$. 

Because fold-level cost variations exist across test splits, Jensen's inequality implies that the expected value of fold-level absolute differences $\text{RFD} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S |\mu_{L,s} - \mu_{H,s}| \ge |\bar{\mu}_L - \bar{\mu}_H|$, where $\bar{\mu}_L = \frac{1}{S}\sum_s \mu_{L,s}$ and $\bar{\mu}_H = \frac{1}{S}\sum_s \mu_{H,s}$ are the macro multi-seed group means. In empirical tables, we report the sample expectation of per-seed group absolute differences $\text{RFD} = 0.1613 \pm 0.1038$, while the macro difference between overall group means is $|\bar{\mu}_L - \bar{\mu}_H| = |0.3408 - 0.2638| = 0.0771$. $\text{RBR}_L$ measures the Recourse Burden Reduction for Low-SES students relative to unconstrained search.

\subsection{Model training, preprocessing, and experimental protocol}
\label{subsec:exp_protocol}

Predictive performance and recourse generation are implemented using a standardized, reproducible experimental protocol:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Classifier Configuration}: The underlying predictor $f(\mathbf{x})$ is a Gradient Boosting Classifier (\texttt{n\_estimators=100}, \texttt{max\_depth=3}, \texttt{learning\_rate=0.1}, \texttt{subsample=1.0}, \texttt{min\_samples\_split=2}, \texttt{loss='log\_loss'}) trained on 7 features ($\text{age}$, $\text{studytime}$, $\text{failures}$, $\text{absences}$, $\text{freetime}$, $\text{goout}$, $\text{SES}$).
    \item \textbf{Preprocessing and Encoding}: Target outcome $y$ is binary pass ($G3 \ge 10$, prevalence $84.6\%$) vs fail ($G3 < 10$, $15.4\%$). Actionable and immutable features are preserved on their natural discrete scales without synthetic oversampling or continuous relaxation.
    \item \textbf{Held-Out Recourse Protocol}: For each of the 10 random seeds ($42 \dots 51$), the dataset ($N=649$) is partitioned into an $80\%$ stratified training split ($N_{\text{train}} \approx 519$) and a $20\%$ test split ($N_{\text{test}} = 130$). Classifiers are trained exclusively on the training fold. Recourse recommendations are generated and evaluated strictly for out-of-fold students predicted to fail in the held-out test fold ($N_{\text{fail}} \approx 12.1$ per test split).

\subsection{Statistical significance testing across random seeds}
\label{subsec:statistical_tests}

To evaluate whether observed cost differences across methods are statistically meaningful, paired two-tailed $t$-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were performed across the 10 random seed test splits ($S=10$). 

Enforcing directional actionability constraints ($\delta_{\text{study}} \ge 0, \delta_{\text{abs}} \le 0$) significantly increased Low-SES recourse cost from $\mu_L = 0.2248$ (Unconstrained) to $\mu_L = 0.3381$ (Actionability-Constrained Search), representing a statistically significant Actionability Cost Premium ($+0.1133$ mean cost increase; paired $t(9) = 3.72, p = 0.0047$; Wilcoxon $W = 0.0, p = 0.0039$). 

Conversely, cost differences between Actionability-Constrained Search ($\mu_L = 0.3381$) and Proposed Difficulty-Weighted Recourse ($\mu_L = 0.3408$, mean diff $+0.0027$, $t(9) = 1.49, p = 0.1708$; Wilcoxon $W = 0.0, p = 0.5000$) were not statistically significant, confirming that friction weighting produced minimal cost impact under standard candidate grids. Similarly, the Manifold nearest-neighbor baseline ($\mu_L = 0.3795$) yielded a higher mean distance ($+0.0413$), but the difference relative to Actionability-Constrained Search was not statistically significant ($t(9) = 1.85, p = 0.0980$; Wilcoxon $W = 8.0, p = 0.0977$). All 10 per-seed fold metrics ($\mu_{L,s}, \mu_{H,s}, |\mu_{L,s}-\mu_{H,s}|$) are exported to \texttt{outputs/tables/heldout\_by\_seed.csv}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The empirical findings clarify the core trade-off between unconstrained numerical optimization and realistic actionability constraints in educational settings.

\subsection{Actionability--Feasibility trade-off and empirical framing}

\hlblock{Our empirical results point to three distinct takeaways:
First, unconstrained counterfactual search achieves lower numerical feature distance ($\mu_L = 0.2248$) because it exploits educationally invalid shortcut directions (e.g., recommending that a student decrease study time or increase absences if the classifier decision boundary allows it).}

\hlblock{Second, enforcing directional actionability constraints ($\delta_{\text{study}} \ge 0, \delta_{\text{abs}} \le 0$) eliminates these invalid shortcuts; numerical action distance necessarily increases ($\mu_L = 0.3381$), representing a statistically significant Actionability Cost Premium ($p = 0.0047$), while recourse validity remains high ($95.5\% \pm 4.7\%$, $11.5 / 12.1$ failing students).}

\hlblock{Third, under the common group-independent evaluation metric, fairness-weighted search selects the exact same candidate actions as actionability-constrained search ($\mu_L = 0.3381$), while proposed fair DAG-informed recourse yields virtually identical feature distance ($\mu_L = 0.3408, p = 0.1708$). Thus, fairness weighting does not produce an empirical cost reduction over actionability constraints alone in this setup. The primary value of the proposed framework lies in eliminating invalid non-causal shortcuts and ensuring high validity under plausible directional bounds, establishing an empirical baseline for recourse feasibility.}

\hlblock{Furthermore, population flow statistics (Table~\ref{tab:population_flow}) show that High-SES students have a high overall pass rate, yielding a small average number of predicted failures per test fold ($N_{\text{fail,High}} = 3.2$ vs. $N_{\text{fail,Low}} = 8.9$). High-SES recourse cost ($\mu_H = 0.2638 \pm 0.1633$) remains stable across Actionability-constrained, Fairness-weighted, and Proposed methods due to this small sample size, which also makes the group disparity metric ($\text{RFD}$) sensitive to minor fold-level variations rather than reflecting a definitive group inequality claim.}

\hlblock{Finally, pre- and post-recourse probability shifts ($0.2866 \to 0.5975$) demonstrate that generated profiles cross the same classifier's decision boundary. It does not show that the recommended behavioral changes would cause real academic improvement. That would require longitudinal or prospective evidence.}

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations should be acknowledged:
1. Feature alignment between UCI and OULAD was too loose (lacking direct study time and absenteeism measures) to justify a quantitative external replication claim; therefore, OULAD was removed from quantitative results tables and positioned as a conceptual framework for future external validation.
2. Causal constraints rely on an assumed educational DAG rather than estimated structural equations.
3. Absences are treated as a historical proxy rather than a direct past intervention.
4. Off-the-shelf equal-K library implementations (CARLA, Karimi, Ustun ILP) were not executed due to continuous optimization and structural causal equation requirements; this remains an empirical limitation of our discrete search setup.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\hlblock{This study establishes an empirical baseline for recourse-oriented student performance analytics, highlighting the fundamental trade-off between unconstrained numerical optimization and directional actionability constraints. By enforcing plausible educational bounds ($\delta_{\text{study}} \ge 0, \delta_{\text{abs}} \le 0$), the framework eliminates invalid shortcut recommendations while maintaining high decision-boundary reversal validity ($95.5\%$). Under a standardized group-independent evaluation metric, fairness weighting did not produce additional empirical cost reductions beyond actionability constraints alone on this dataset. These findings demonstrate that model-based recourse in education should be evaluated through realistic feasibility constraints rather than unconstrained numerical minimizations, providing a foundation for responsible educational decision support.}
